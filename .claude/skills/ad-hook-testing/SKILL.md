# Ad Hook Testing

The goal here is not "run ads for the game." It is to build the one skill that
transfers to every product you will ever sell. Platform mechanics change every
18 months and are learnable in an afternoon. **Hook writing and honest
measurement are the durable skills.** Spend your attention there.

## What actually transfers

| Transfers completely | Dies with the platform |
|---|---|
| Writing a hook that stops a scroll | Which button says "Boost" this year |
| Knowing what a result at n=40 can mean | TikTok's 2026 bid strategy names |
| Naming the one variable a test isolates | Ad Manager's UI layout |
| Reading CTR without fooling yourself | Placement taxonomies |
| Matching promise → landing page | Pixel install steps |

When a task is about the right column, do it quickly and move on. When it is
about the left column, slow down — that is the part being learned.

## Writing a hook

A hook earns attention in about one second. Four families cover nearly
everything; each fails in a specific way.

1. **Tension** — sets up a conflict the viewer wants resolved.
   *"They tried to tax the vibe."* Fails when the tension is invented and the
   viewer feels tricked.
2. **Plain value** — states the offer flatly.
   *"Free browser game. No download."* Fails by being forgettable, never by
   being unclear. This is the control every other hook must beat.
3. **Identity** — names the viewer.
   *"POV: you found the free game at 2am."* Fails when the viewer doesn't
   recognise themselves; strongest on TikTok, weakest in search.
4. **Specificity** — a concrete, checkable detail.
   *"Built in Godot in 30 days."* Fails when the number is boring. Specific
   numbers beat adjectives almost always.

Rules that hold across all four:

- **One idea per hook.** Two ideas is zero ideas.
- **Concrete beats clever.** "No wallet needed" outperforms "frictionless Web3."
- **Front-load the surprising word.** Attention decays left to right.
- **Never write a hook you cannot back up on the landing page.** A hook that
  overpromises buys a click and loses the customer — and on a real product,
  refunds cost more than the click did.

## Structuring a test that can be read

The most common way to waste a budget is running two ads that differ in three
ways. When the result comes back you learn nothing, because you cannot say
which difference caused it.

**Change exactly one variable per test.** Everything else identical: same
image, same audience, same placement, same landing page, same budget, same
days. If the image and the headline both change, the test is dead on arrival.

Test in this order — earlier items have far bigger effect sizes:

1. **Hook / headline** (biggest lever, cheapest to change)
2. **Creative format** (static vs video vs UGC)
3. **Audience**
4. **Landing page**
5. Bid strategy, placements, everything else (marginal — stop here unless
   spending real money)

## What each budget can actually teach

Be honest about this or the whole exercise becomes superstition.

| Budget | Clicks (approx) | The one question it can answer |
|---|---|---|
| **$10** | 10–40 | Almost nothing statistically. Treat as a *mechanics rehearsal*: does the account work, does tracking fire, does the funnel connect end to end. |
| **$50–100** | 50–200 | Directional hook comparison, if one wins by a wide margin. |
| **$300+** | 300–1,000 | A real CTR read on 2–3 hooks. |
| **$1,000+** | — | Conversion-rate and audience tests become meaningful. |

At n=40, a 2% vs 3% CTR difference is noise — that is one extra click. The
useful rule of thumb: **if the winner isn't ahead by roughly 50% relative at
small n, you learned nothing about the hooks.** Say so plainly rather than
manufacturing a narrative from noise. Learning to *not* over-read a small
result is itself one of the highest-value skills here.

## What a $10 run is genuinely for

Reframe it as a dress rehearsal, and it becomes worth every cent:

- Prove the ad account, billing, and approval flow work.
- Prove UTMs fire and land in analytics.
- See a real CPM for this audience — that single number is what you use to
  budget every future campaign.
- Discover policy rejections *before* they matter.

Those four outcomes are worth far more than 40 clicks, and they are exactly
what you need working before a product with real margin goes live.

## Reading results without fooling yourself

- **CTR** is the hook's score. It is the only metric a tiny test can inform.
- **CPM** tells you what the audience costs, not whether the ad is good.
- **Conversion rate** needs hundreds of clicks. Do not read it below that.
- **Never compare across platforms.** A 4% TikTok CTR and a 1% X CTR are
  different games with different denominators.

Always write down the prediction *before* looking at results. If you cannot
say in advance which variant you expect to win and why, the test is not
measuring an idea — and calibrating that prediction is how judgment is built.

## Organic is the free hook lab

This is the highest-leverage habit in the whole skill, especially at low
budget: **organic posts test hooks for free, at higher volume than a small ad
budget buys.**

Post the same creative with three different first lines across three days.
The one with the best view-through and comment ratio is your paid hook. Now
the ad budget is spent on a hook that already has evidence behind it, instead
of buying the evidence itself.

This is the actual professional workflow — "paid organic." Cold paid ads
should be the *last* step, not the first.

## Carrying this to a product you sell

Everything above holds. What changes:

- **The metric moves** from CTR to cost per acquisition, then to LTV/CAC. CTR
  becomes a diagnostic, not a goal.
- **Landing-page match becomes critical.** With a free game, a mismatched
  promise costs a bounce. With a paid product it costs a refund and a review.
- **You can finally afford real tests,** because a product with margin funds
  the sample sizes that make results trustworthy.
- **Claim discipline stops being a style rule and becomes legal exposure.**
  The habit of only writing claims you can substantiate — practised here on a
  free game — is the habit that keeps a real business out of trouble.

That last point is why the accuracy rules on this project are worth respecting
even though nothing is being sold yet. You are rehearsing the discipline on
low stakes so it is automatic when the stakes are real.
