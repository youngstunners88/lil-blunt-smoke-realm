# Revenue Paths

## The thing to say first

**There is currently nothing to buy.** The game is free, the site sells
nothing, and no page has a price on it. Every marketing improvement — better
ads, #1 rankings, more traffic — multiplies against zero.

```
traffic × $0 = $0
```

This is not a reason to stop marketing; the audience and the skills are real
assets. But no amount of SEO or ad spend produces income until something is
purchasable or a third party pays for the attention. Say this plainly whenever
"how do we make money from this" comes up, before proposing tactics.

The second thing to say: **"passive" describes the income, never the setup.**
Every path below takes real work up front. What separates them is whether that
work keeps paying after it is done.

## Ranked by how directly they convert what already exists

### 1. Web game portals — the only path that needs no new product

Portals like CrazyGames pay developers a share of ad revenue for HTML5 games.
This turns the game that already exists into income without building anything
new, which is why it ranks first. Verified terms as of August 2026:

- Upload the HTML5/WebGL build through the developer portal. **No fee, no
  exclusivity requirement** — it can stay on itch.io and anywhere else.
- QA review, then **Basic Launch** (listed, no SDK work). If it holds player
  attention, you are invited to **Full Launch** — integrate the SDK and begin
  earning.
- Revenue share published for their 2026 game jam: **60% of in-game ad
  revenue, 70% of in-game purchases**. The main developer docs do not publish
  a split, so confirm the live terms before counting on a number.
- Optional 2-month launch exclusivity raises the share by 50%.
- Monthly payout via Tipalti, **€100 minimum balance**.

The honest catch: earnings scale with plays, and Full Launch is gated on
retention. A game nobody finishes earns nothing. This path rewards making the
game genuinely good far more than it rewards marketing it.

Effort: days. Yield: real but play-dependent. Passive once live.

### 2. itch.io donations / pay-what-you-want

Already published there; enabling a tip jar is a settings change. Effort:
minutes. Yield: small, and highly dependent on an emotionally invested
audience. Worth doing because it costs nothing, not because it will pay.

### 3. Print-on-demand merch

The character art is genuinely strong, and print-on-demand is structurally the
purest passive income available: upload once, no inventory, no shipping, no
fulfilment. Redbubble/TeePublic are fully hands-off at lower margin; Printful
plus a storefront pays more per unit for more setup.

**The trap to name out loud:** merch monetizes an audience that already exists.
It does not create one. Merch for an unknown brand sells approximately nothing,
and the failure feels like a marketing problem when it is a sequencing problem.
This becomes a real path *after* there is a fanbase, not before.

Effort: a day. Yield: near zero until there is an audience, then genuinely
passive.

### 4. Developer content → a digital product

The highest ceiling and the slowest. The Tier 3 queries in
`search-ranking-strategy` (deploying Godot to the Internet Computer, building
Web3 games without a wallet) are winnable *and* attract an audience with money
and budget. Build that audience with genuinely novel first-hand content, then
sell a boilerplate, template or course to it.

This is the path that most resembles the stated long-term goal — advertising a
product with margin — and the advertising skills being built on the game
transfer to it directly.

Effort: 6–12 months. Yield: highest ceiling, fully passive once the product
exists.

### 5. Affiliate / partner-protocol referrals

Possible with the `$GOLD` and `$DIAMONDS` partner protocols, but constrained:
the accuracy rules in `AGENTS.md` prohibit implying that playing earns tokens.
Any referral arrangement has to be disclosed and must not blur into "play to
earn" messaging. Treat as low priority and check the copy carefully.

## The sequencing that follows from all this

1. **Portals first** — it is the only option that monetizes what already exists.
2. **Make the game more retentive** — under a revenue-share model, retention
   *is* the business model. This is where dev effort pays best.
3. **Keep building the ad skill** on a free product where mistakes are cheap
   (`ad-learning-loop`).
4. **Merch once there is an audience** to sell to.
5. **The developer product** as the real long-term play.

## What not to do

- Do not spend on ads to drive traffic to a free game and call it an
  investment. At present it buys learning, not return — which is a fine reason
  to do it, but name it correctly.
- Do not build a store before there is an audience. An empty shop is worse than
  no shop.
- Do not add token or earnings claims to make the project look monetized. It
  breaks the standing accuracy rule, and it is the fastest way to lose an ad
  account and a domain reputation.
